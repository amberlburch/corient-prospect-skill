# Where the first line comes from

The playbook says the opening line explains 25 to 30% of all reply-rate variance, and that it
must be about them, verifiable, and impossible to send to anyone else. It does not say where to
look. This file does.

Six sources, ranked. Work down the list and stop at the first one that yields something real.
A weak signal from bucket 1 beats a strong signal from bucket 6, because it proves you read
rather than filtered.

---

## 1. Self-authored content

**What it is.** Anything they made: a post, a talk, a podcast appearance, a newsletter, a
conference panel, a product changelog they wrote, a job ad with their fingerprints on it.

**Where to look.** LinkedIn activity tab (posts, not reposts), company blog bylines, YouTube and
podcast guest appearances, Substack, conference programmes, GitHub for technical founders.

**What it licenses you to say.** Their actual argument, in their words, disagreed with or
extended. This is the only bucket where you can take a position and have it land as a peer.

**Failure mode.** Praising the post instead of engaging with it. "Loved your piece on
attribution" is bucket 1 data spent as a bucket 4 compliment. Quote the claim, then say what it
made you think.

---

## 2. Engaged content

**What it is.** What they commented on, shared with a note, or reacted to. Reveals the topic
they are chewing on right now, which is often ahead of what they have published.

**Where to look.** LinkedIn activity tab, comments section under industry posts, their reposts
with commentary.

**What it licenses you to say.** The topic, framed as a live question rather than a settled
view. "You were in the thread on iOS attribution last week" is fair. Attributing a position to
them from a reaction is not.

**Failure mode.** Treating a like as an endorsement. A reaction is evidence of attention, not
agreement.

---

## 3. Self-identified traits

**What it is.** How they describe themselves: headline, About section, the verbs in their role
description, the way they name their own function.

**Where to look.** LinkedIn headline and About, company team page bio, speaker bios.

**What it licenses you to say.** Their own language, reused. If they call it "demand" and you
call it "lead gen", you have told them you are outside their world. This is the cheapest
credibility available and almost nobody spends it.

**Failure mode.** Quoting the headline back at them verbatim. Use the vocabulary, not the
sentence.

---

## 4. Personal details

**What it is.** Hobbies, volunteer work, languages, a sport, a hometown, an unusual career jump.

**Where to look.** LinkedIn Interests and Volunteering, personal site, an About page with
personality in it.

**What it licenses you to say.** Almost nothing on a first touch. This bucket is warmth, and
warmth without relevance reads as either research theatre or surveillance.

**Failure mode.** Using it as the opener. If the first line is about their marathon and the
second is about their pipeline, the join is visible and the whole message reads assembled.
Reserve for a genuine shared thing, and only ever as an aside.

---

## 5. Professional background

**What it is.** Tenure, career trajectory, prior companies, board seats, awards, the shape of
the team they have built.

**Where to look.** LinkedIn experience, Crunchbase, company announcements, industry awards.

**What it licenses you to say.** A hypothesis about their situation grounded in position rather
than personality. "Eighteen months in and the second growth hire just landed" is a real
observation about where a company sits.

**Failure mode.** Tenure flattery ("15 years in the category"). The playbook is explicit that
recent specific proof beats tenure: swapping "15 years placing finance leaders" for "placed a
CFO into a similar platform, closed in 9 weeks" moved one campaign from 6.1% to 9.8%.

---

## 6. Company level

**What it is.** Funding, hiring pattern, product launch, M&A, market entry, website messaging
changes, tech stack, published financials.

**Where to look.** See `triggers.md`. This bucket is where the trigger library lives.

**What it licenses you to say.** Timing. A company-level fact rarely personalises a message on
its own, but it is the strongest possible reason for the message to arrive today rather than
any other day.

**Failure mode.** Stopping here and calling it personalised. Company-level data is available to
every competitor running the same filter, so on its own it proves nothing about attention. It
works stacked: the playbook records two stacked signals at 4.7x positive replies.

---

## When there is nothing

Do not invent, and do not stretch a bucket 6 fact into a bucket 1 claim.

Use a **segment frame**, stated as a hypothesis with the uncertainty visible:

> Most founder-led companies at your stage are running paid and organic through different
> people, so the numbers never reconcile. Might be wrong about yours.

Three rules for the fallback:

1. The frame must be true of the whole segment, so it is honest even when it misses.
2. The uncertainty is stated, not hedged away. "Might be wrong" is doing real work.
3. The draft note must record that personalisation was limited, so the campaign read is not
   later attributed to copy quality.

The playbook's own P.S. finding applies here: stating the honest targeting criteria
("we're looking for firms X years old with a reputation like yours") lifts believability and
positive-reply share. If you cannot personalise, explaining why you picked them is the next
best thing.

---

## Hard boundary

Nothing in any message asserts a fact about the recipient's own internal setup. You may state
what is published, and you may state a hypothesis marked as one. You may not state what their
stack, spend, team or numbers are. This is a house rule from the claims register and it is not
negotiable, because a confident wrong claim about their business kills the thread and the
account.
